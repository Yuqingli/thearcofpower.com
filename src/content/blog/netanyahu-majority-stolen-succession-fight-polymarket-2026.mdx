Polymarket's [Israel next-PM market](https://polymarket.com/event/who-will-be-the-next-prime-minister-of-israel-after-the-next-election) — $70 million in total volume, the platform's most-traded geopolitics contract — settled into a quiet verdict on Wednesday night: **Netanyahu 42%, Gadi Eizenkot 34%, everyone else in single digits.**

That 8-point gap looks comfortable until you remember where it was 48 hours earlier. Before exit polls dropped on election night, Eizenkot was the favorite at 56%. Netanyahu sat at 27.5%. The exit polls [swung the line 15 points in Netanyahu's favor overnight](https://www.i24news.tv/en/news/israel-election-s-2026/artc-turnaround-at-polymarket-after-the-exit-polls-netanyahu-leads-in-the-odds-to-become-prime-minister) — and then it stopped. The market priced in the partial count, absorbed the "stolen election" rhetoric, and landed on 42%.

Not a coronation. Not a collapse. A succession fight — priced in real time by capital that does not care about ideology.

## The Numbers That Don't Lie

Israel's Central Elections Committee, with [over 4 million votes counted](https://www.jpost.com/israel-election-2026/2026-10-28/live-updates-913081), put Netanyahu's right-religious bloc at **54 seats** — seven short of the 61 needed to govern. Eizenkot's opposition coalition held roughly 48, with Arab-led parties accounting for the remaining 18-19 seats. [Neither bloc has a majority](https://www.hurriyetdailynews.com/netanyahu-bloc-leads-partial-count-but-lacks-majority-228696). Neither can form a government alone.

The Polymarket read is more precise than any poll because it prices coalition probability, not vote share. At 42%, the market says Netanyahu is the most likely individual to end up as PM — but the probability that *someone other than Netanyahu* becomes PM is **58%**. That is the story the headline misses.

## The "Stolen" Playbook

Within hours of partial results showing his bloc short of a majority, [Likud released a statement](https://www.i24news.tv/en/news/israeli-elections/artc-likud-claims-election-stolen-demands-probe-into-alleged-voting-fraud) alleging that Arab parties had "stolen" the election through "bribery, forged ballots, voter intimidation, and illegal software use across multiple polling stations." The party said it was handing evidence to three separate bodies: the Israeli Police, the Shin Bet, and the Central Elections Committee.

The parallel is not subtle, and it is not accidental. This is a playbook with a precise tactical purpose:

**1. Delegitimize the count.** By targeting Arab voting stations specifically, Likud frames any opposition majority that depends on Arab parties as structurally illegitimate. This is not about proving fraud — it is about poisoning the well of coalition math.

**2. Buy time.** Official results are due November 4. The longer the count is contested, the more time Netanyahu has to negotiate with potential coalition partners — or to pressure wavering members of the religious right to stay loyal.

**3. Set up the snap-election option.** If no coalition forms within the mandated timeline, Israel goes back to the polls. Netanyahu has survived six elections. A seventh is not a threat to him — it is a feature. The "stolen" narrative ensures his base stays mobilized for the next round.

But the market did not swing all the way. It settled at 42/34 — not the 55/30 that a Netanyahu-wins narrative would produce, and not the 30/56 that a clean opposition victory would show. The market is pricing a specific scenario: **a prolonged succession fight with no clean resolution.**

Here is what the 42/34 split encodes:

- **Netanyahu remains the most probable individual PM** — but at less than coin-flip odds
- **The probability of a non-Netanyahu government is 58%** — the market sees his era as more likely ending than continuing
- **The gap is closing** — in our July analysis, the market was 38-38; the election moved it to 42-34; Netanyahu gained ground but not enough to exit the danger zone
- **Katz at 2%** confirms the race is binary — this is Netanyahu vs. Eizenkot, with no third candidate viable

## The Eisenkot Dilemma: Arab Votes Without Arab Partners

The [Arab Center DC published a crucial analysis](https://arabcenterdc.org/resource/israels-election-and-eisenkots-arab-coalition-dilemma/) this week on Israel's election and Eisenkot's Arab coalition dilemma. The problem is arithmetic.

Eizenkot's opposition bloc holds roughly 48 seats. To reach 61, he needs Arab-led parties — who control 18-19 seats and could comfortably deliver a majority. But Eizenkot has publicly ruled out a formal coalition with Arab parties. The political cost in Israeli Jewish politics would be terminal.

The workaround is a minority government sustained by external Arab support — Arab lawmakers vote confidence but do not join the cabinet. It has historical precedent (Rabin's government in the 1990s relied on similar arrangements). But it is inherently unstable. Any withdrawal of Arab support collapses the government.

This is the variable the prediction market is pricing. At 34%, Eizenkot is priced not as unlikely-to-win but as likely-to-face-a-fragile-arrangement. The market is saying: he can probably assemble a government, but it might not survive its first crisis.

Netanyahu's counterplay is patience. If Eizenkot cannot form a stable coalition within the mandated period, the mandate passes back — potentially to Netanyahu, or to a new election where the "stolen" narrative has had weeks to marinate in his base.

## Three Lessons This Succession Fight Teaches

**Lesson 1: Prediction markets lead media on political transitions by days, sometimes weeks.**

In July, when Israeli media was still framing the election as "Netanyahu vs. Bennett," Polymarket had already priced Eizenkot as the co-favorite. The [38-38 dead heat we reported](/blog/netanyahu-18-point-collapse-israel-leadership-polymarket-2026) appeared in Israeli polling only weeks later. Today, the market prices a succession fight while most English-language coverage still frames the story as "Netanyahu claims fraud." The market has moved past the noise to the structural question: who assembles 61 seats?

**Lesson 2: "Stolen election" rhetoric is a position, not a belief.**

Netanyahu does not need to prove fraud. He needs to delay resolution, energize his base, and delegitimize any coalition that includes Arab support. The rhetoric tracks precisely with Trump's 2020 playbook — not because Netanyahu copied it, but because it solves the same problem: how does a powerful leader who lost an election avoid a clean transfer of power? The answer, in both cases, is to make the transfer messy enough that the alternative looks unstable. The [Times of Israel asked directly](https://www.timesofisrael.com/netanyahu-claims-his-knesset-majority-was-stolen-from-him-does-that-add-up/): "Does that add up?" The answer from the Central Elections Committee: no.

**Lesson 3: The Arab vote is the actual variable, and everyone is pretending it is not.**

Every analysis of Israeli coalition math eventually arrives at the same conclusion: the Arab parties hold the balance of power. Eizenkot needs them. Netanyahu needs to prevent them from being counted. The "stolen election" rhetoric targeting Arab polling stations is not random — it is strategically aimed at the exact parties whose support would make an Eizenkot government viable. If Likud can frame Arab participation as fraudulent, it raises the political cost of any arrangement that depends on Arab votes.

## The Road from Here

Official results arrive November 4. The president will then begin consultations, asking each party to recommend a candidate for prime minister. The candidate who receives the most recommendations gets 28 days to form a coalition, extendable by 14.

If that candidate fails, the president can offer the mandate to someone else. If no one succeeds, Israel goes to its seventh election in eight years.

The Polymarket odds will update in real time through every stage. Watch the Eizenkot line: if it crosses 40%, the market is pricing a successful coalition formation. If Netanyahu climbs above 50%, the market sees either a coalition breakthrough or a snap election where he is favored.

At 42/34, the market's current message is clear: this is not over. The succession fight the media has not yet framed is already priced.
